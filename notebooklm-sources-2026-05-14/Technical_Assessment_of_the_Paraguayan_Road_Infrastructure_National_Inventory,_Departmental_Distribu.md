Technical Assessment of the Paraguayan Road Infrastructure: National Inventory, Departmental Distribution, and Rural Connectivity Metrics (2017-2018)

The landlocked status of the Republic of Paraguay necessitates an internal transport framework that is not merely functional but optimized for international competitiveness and domestic territorial integration. In the period spanning 2017 to 2018, the Ministry of Public Works and Communications (MOPC) undertook a significant institutional and physical modernization of the national road network, a transition fueled by the need to lower logistical costs and fulfill the objectives of the National Development Plan 2030.\[1, 2, 3\] Historically, Paraguay’s infrastructure had suffered from a legacy of underinvestment, where as late as 2010, nearly 90% of the 60,000 kilometers of roads were unpaved, leaving the nation’s agricultural output highly vulnerable to climatic conditions.\[2\] By 2018, the total inventoried network had expanded to 80,127 kilometers, reflecting a concerted effort to map, categorize, and improve the vast rural reaches of the country.\[3\]

Macro-Economic Context and the Logistical Imperative

Paraguay’s economic profile is defined by its role as a primary exporter of soybeans, beef, and energy, with trade representing 71.08% of its Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in 2017.\[4, 5\] The efficiency of the road network is the primary determinant of the "last-mile" cost for these commodities. In 2017, the country achieved an annual GDP growth rate of 4.8%, despite significant logistical hurdles.\[5\] The reliance on terrestrial transport is absolute; in the absence of a functional freight rail system, 88.7% of the region’s natural resource exports are moved by road.\[6, 7\] Consequently, any degradation in the road surface or lack of all-season accessibility directly impacts the national balance of payments by increasing fuel consumption, vehicle maintenance costs, and travel times.\[2, 8\]

The period of 2017-2018 was also marked by a shift in how infrastructure was funded and managed. The MOPC moved toward a "level of service" model for maintenance, supported by the World Bank and the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB).\[1\] This involved the implementation of CREMA (Contrato de Rehabilitación y Mantenimiento) and GMANS (Gestión de Mantenimiento por Niveles de Servicio) contracts, which covered over 60% of the paved network by late 2016.\[9\] Under these contracts, the government ceased paying for specific inputs and began paying for results, such as pavement smoothness, signal visibility, and drainage functionality.\[1, 9\] This institutional maturation was a prerequisite for the large-scale inventory update that occurred at the end of 2018.\[3\]

Quantitative Analysis of the National Road Inventory (2018)

The culmination of the 2017-2018 biennium was the official update of the National Road Registry, the first such comprehensive revision in 57 years.\[3\] This update, formalized in late 2018 and early 2019, identified a total network of 80,127 kilometers.\[3\] This inventory categorized the network into three primary classifications: National Routes, Departmental Routes, and Rural/Vecinal Roads.\[3, 10\]

The data reveals that while the absolute length of paved roads has grown—reaching nearly 7,000 kilometers—the vast majority of the network (over 88%) remains composed of earth tracks.\[3\] This creates a bifurcated infrastructure reality: a high-speed primary network that is 91% paved, and a massive rural network that is less than 0.5% paved.\[12\] The "Improved" category, encompassing *empedrados* and gravel, serves as the vital intermediary layer that prevents total regional isolation during heavy rains.\[3, 13\]

The 2018 Reclassification of National Routes

The 2018 reform redefined the hierarchy of the road network to prioritize the 22 National Routes (PY01 through PY22).\[3, 11\] These routes are the arteries of the nation, connecting the capital to departmental seats, international borders, and strategic ports.\[3\] Prior to 2018, the system was centered on a smaller number of routes that had become insufficient for the country’s current demographic and economic reality.\[3\]

The implications of this reclassification are profound for the Chaco region (Western Region). Previously, only two national routes (IX and XII) served the Chaco; the 2018 system expanded this to six routes (PY09, PY12, PY14, PY15, PY16, and PY05 partially), representing a strategic commitment to the integration of the trans-border corridors.\[3, 11\] In the Eastern Region, the national routes grew from a paved total of 3,494 kilometers to 5,502 kilometers, reflecting the formalization of high-traffic departmental segments into the national priority list.\[3\]

Departmental Distribution of Road Infrastructure

The distribution of road types across Paraguay's 17 departments exhibits a clear correlation between economic density and infrastructure development. The Eastern Region, home to the majority of the population and the intensive soybean and meat production clusters, possesses the highest concentration of paved routes.\[5, 14\]

Paved Infrastructure Ranking by Department (2018)

Official MOPC reports and construction guild data (CAVIALPA) indicate that the department of Itapúa serves as the national leader in paved road length, with more than 1,000 kilometers of paved routes.\[14\] This is followed in order of magnitude by:

- **Itapúa:** Over 1,000 km (Strategic grain corridor/PY01/PY06).- **Central:** High urban density and metropolitan connectivity (PY01/PY02/PY03).- **San Pedro:** Critical agricultural expansion zone (PY03/PY08/PY11).- **Caaguazú:** The "geographic center" of the Eastern Region (PY02/PY08/PY13).\[14\]

The Rural Access Index (RAI) and Sustainable Development Goals

The Rural Access Index (RAI), adopted as UN Sustainable Development Goal Indicator 9.1.1, measures the proportion of the rural population living within 2 kilometers (approximately a 20-25 minute walk) of an all-season road.\[15, 16\] For Paraguay, this indicator is the most accurate reflection of social equity in infrastructure.\[2, 17\]

In 2019, using datasets gathered during the 2017-2018 period, the World Bank calculated the RAI for Paraguay at 42.4%.\[18, 19\] This suggests that while significant progress has been made, 57.6% of the rural population—roughly 1.3 million people—still lacks reliable year-round access to schools, healthcare, and markets.\[2, 5, 20\]

Methodology and Geospatial Determinants of the RAI

The calculation of the 42.4% figure for Paraguay utilized the 2016 World Bank methodology, which leverages geospatial data rather than traditional household surveys.\[15, 16\] The process involved:

- **Population Distribution:** Using WorldPop data to estimate population density at a 100x100 meter resolution.\[15, 16\]- **Road Network Mapping:** Integrating MOPC official databases with OpenStreetMap (OSM) to capture both official routes and unofficial rural tracks.\[15, 16\]- **Urban/Rural Masking:** Utilizing the Global Rural-Urban Mapping Project (GRUMP) to exclude urban populations already served by municipal streets.\[15, 16\]- **Buffer Analysis:** Creating a 2km buffer around all roads designated as "all-season" (paved, empedrado, or high-quality gravel) and counting the rural residents within that buffer.\[15, 21\]

The findings indicated that in departments like Itapúa and Central, the RAI was significantly higher due to the dense network of *empedrados*.\[14\] However, in departments like San Pedro and Caaguazú, the "all-season" designation was often compromised by the poor state of the earth roads, which, despite being mapped, are frequently rendered unusable by rainfall.\[2, 13\]

Institutional Management: Maintenance and Expenditure

The maintenance of the road network in 2017-2018 was managed through a sophisticated hierarchy of contracts. The MOPC achieved a coverage of 35% to 60% of the paved network through performance-based models.\[9\]

Maintenance Framework by Tier (2018 Estimates)

| Management Model | Coverage (km) | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **CREMA Contracts** | 3,202.50 | 5-year contracts for rehabilitation and multi-year maintenance |
| **GMANS/Level of Service** | ~1,200 | Payment based on International Roughness Index (IRI) standards |
| **Concessions** | 144.00 | Private sector management of high-traffic segments (e.g., Tape Porã) |

*(Source: MOPC Strategy and Planning Documents 2016-2018 \[1, 9\])*

The CREMA and GMANS programs were instrumental in maintaining the 93% compliance rate for road quality indicators on the primary network.\[1\] However, the 2019 Expenditure Review noted that while the primary network was well-funded, the maintenance of the 70,000 kilometers of earth roads was chronically underfunded, often relying on "vecinal" committees and local municipalities with limited equipment.\[12, 22\]

Sectoral Expenditures and Fiscal Sustainability

Public spending on roads in Paraguay saw a dramatic increase during the 2017-2018 biennium. In 2017, GDP per capita was estimated at $5,335, and by 2018, it rose to $5,805.\[5\] This growth provided the fiscal space for the government to invest heavily in non-financial assets, which reached 2.9% of GDP by 2019.\[23\] The funding for these works was derived from three primary sources:

- **Sovereign Bonds:** Used for major national routes like the Transchaco and the Bioceanic corridor.\[12\]- **Binational Royalties:** Funds from the Itaipú and Yacyretá hydroelectric dams channeled into regional infrastructure.\[23\]- **Multilateral Loans:** World Bank and IDB support specifically targeted at rural connectivity and institutional strengthening.\[1, 12\]

Engineering Standards and Climate Resilience

The technical specifications of the road network in 2018 were guided by the updated MOPC Highway Manual, which introduced more stringent requirements for pavement durability.\[8\] This was a direct response to the increasing weight of transport vehicles, which often exceeded the 15-ton nominal limits.\[8\]

Tropical Hydrology and Drainage Infrastructure

The 2017-2018 projects emphasized the replacement of aged wooden bridges with concrete structures. In the Eastern Region, this included thousands of linear meters of box culverts and bridges, such as the Yukyry stream bridge which spans 120 meters with four 30-meter spans supported by 1500 mm diameter piles.\[8\] These interventions are essential for achieving "all-season" status, as even a perfectly paved road is useless if its drainage cross-sections are overwhelmed by tropical storms.\[2\]

Socio-Economic Impact of Targeted 2017-2018 Projects

The MOPC’s "Rural Transformation" program in 2017 targeted several high-impact corridors that serve as case studies for the broader infrastructure strategy.\[24\]

Case Study 1: The Ruta de la Banana (Caaguazú)

This project encompassed 41.6 kilometers connecting Tembiaporã, Raúl Arsenio Oviedo, and José Domingo Ocampos.\[24\] As of 2018, this was a critical "rent crop" corridor. The transition from earth to pavement was estimated to reduce the loss of perishable produce (bananas and cassava) by 30%, as the "roughness" of unpaved roads (measured by the International Roughness Index) previously caused significant bruising and damage to the fruit during transit.\[2, 13, 24\]

Case Study 2: Quyquyhó – María Antonia (Paraguarí)

Spanning 31.5 kilometers, this paving project was designed to connect isolated rural communities to the primary PY01 route.\[24\] The socio-economic impact was measured not just in trade volumes but in service accessibility; the time to reach the nearest regional hospital was reduced from 90 minutes to 25 minutes, directly influencing the local infant mortality and maternal health metrics.\[20, 24\]

Case Study 3: O'Leary – San Cristóbal (Alto Paraná)

Covering 62.29 kilometers, this project targeted the "true economic engine" of the country—the agricultural cooperatives of Alto Paraná.\[24\] By providing an all-season link to the PY06 and PY02, this route ensured that the soybean harvest could be transported to the Paraná River ports regardless of the season, stabilizing the cash flow for thousands of small and medium-scale producers.\[24, 25\]

Analysis of the Chaco Logistics Framework

The Chaco (Western Region) represents 60% of Paraguay’s land area but contains less than 5% of its population and a similarly small fraction of its paved roads as of 2018.\[3, 5\] The 2018 reclassification and the subsequent "Bioceanic Corridor" project (PY15) sought to fundamentally change this dynamic.\[11\]

The Transchaco (PY09) Rehabilitation

As of 2018, the PY09 (Transchaco) was in a state of severe degradation, with over 700 kilometers requiring total reconstruction.\[9\] This route is the sole lifeline for the central Chaco’s dairy and beef colonies (Loma Plata, Filadelfia). The rehabilitation project, funded by the IDB and the Development Bank of Latin America (CAF), was designed to support an axle load of 10 tons per wheel, anticipating the heavy cattle transport that defines the region’s economy.\[3, 11\]

The Bioceanic Corridor (PY15)

The 531-kilometer PY15 route, starting at Carmelo Peralta on the Brazilian border, was the most ambitious project initiated in the 2017-2018 period.\[3, 11\] By creating a paved link across the northern Chaco, Paraguay aimed to capture a portion of the transit trade between Brazil’s Mato Grosso and the Chilean ports of Antofagasta and Iquique.\[25\] This project reflects a second-order insight: Paraguay is transitioning from a "landlocked" mindset to a "land-linked" strategy, where infrastructure is designed for regional transit rather than just internal consumption.\[1, 7, 25\]

Conclusion: Synthesis of the 2018 Infrastructure Status

The technical and quantitative data from the 2017-2018 period paints a picture of a nation in the midst of a foundational infrastructural overhaul. The total road network of 80,127 kilometers is characterized by a highly efficient, 91% paved primary system that successfully facilitates the bulk of national exports.\[3, 12\] However, the departmental analysis reveals persistent inequalities, with the rural earth network (70,643 km) remaining the weak link in the national value chain.\[3, 12\]

The Rural Access Index of 42.4% serves as both a baseline and a challenge for future administrations.\[18, 19\] While the "Rural Transformation" projects in Caaguazú, Itapúa, and Paraguarí have demonstrated the high ROI of paving strategic rural segments, the sheer scale of the unpaved network (88.16%) requires a continued shift toward cost-effective "improved" surfaces like gravel and *empedrados*.\[2, 3, 24\]

Ultimately, the 2018 reclassification of the 22 National Routes provided the institutional framework necessary to manage this complexity. By clearly defining the hierarchy of the network, the MOPC and its international partners have established a prioritized investment pipeline that balances the need for international logistical hubs (like the Bioceanic Corridor) with the imperative of rural social inclusion (as measured by the RAI).\[3, 11, 17, 24\] The success of these initiatives will be determined by the sustainability of the maintenance models (CREMA/GMANS) and the continued fiscal commitment to closing the "access gap" that still leaves more than half of the rural population vulnerable to the elements.\[1, 2, 9, 18\]
