# The 2018 Presidential Campaign of the Asociación Nacional Republicana: An Exhaustive Analysis of Financial Architecture, Logistical Mobilization, and Electoral Integrity

# The 2018 Presidential Campaign of the Asociación Nacional Republicana: An Exhaustive Analysis of Financial Architecture, Logistical Mobilization, and Electoral Integrity

The general elections held in the Republic of Paraguay on April 22, 2018, represented a significant juncture in the nation’s democratic trajectory, particularly regarding the internal dynamics and external projection of the Asociación Nacional Republicana (ANR), commonly known as the Colorado Party. Under the leadership of Mario Abdo Benítez, the 2018 campaign served as a case study in the intersection of traditional clientelistic structures and evolving institutional oversight. This report provides an exhaustive investigation into the campaign’s financial foundations, the massive scale of its logistical operations, the mobilization of its field staff, and the strategic deployment of its territorial rallies, synthesized from official declarations to the Superior Tribunal of Electoral Justice (TSJE), international observer reports from the European Union (EU) and the Organization of American States (OAS), and investigative journalism.

## The Institutional Landscape and Legal Architecture of the 2018 Campaign

The 2018 electoral process was governed by a legal framework that was in the midst of a gradual and often contested transition toward greater transparency. At the center of this framework was the Paraguayan Electoral Code, Law No. 834/1996, which had been modified by Law No. 4.743/2012 to regulate political financing.[1] This legislative structure defined the parameters for both public and private funding, establishing the "subsidio electoral" as a post-election reimbursement based on the number of votes obtained, and the "aporte estatal" as an annual contribution for party maintenance.[1]

However, the efficacy of these regulations in 2018 was frequently questioned by international bodies. The Organization of American States (OAS) noted that while the legal framework provided a basis for reporting, it lacked the necessary teeth to combat the penetration of illicit funds, particularly from organized crime and drug trafficking, which have historically sought to influence the political class.[2] The 2018 campaign operated under a system where the TSJE exercised a formalistic oversight, primarily focused on the mathematical reconciliation of receipts rather than the investigative auditing of the origin of funds. This gap was only partially addressed in subsequent years through the enactment of Laws 6.167/18 and 6.501/20, which sought to introduce real-time traceability during internal party primaries—a direct response to the vulnerabilities exposed during the 2018 cycle.[1, 3]

The 2018 campaign was also characterized by a unique delegation of power regarding media access. While the law provided for free electoral advertising space, the TSJE delegated the actual allocation of this time to the media outlets themselves, a practice that the OAS identified as a potential source of inequity.[5] This institutional arrangement favored established parties like the ANR, which possessed the pre-existing relationships and organizational weight to negotiate favorable placements in the media landscape.

## Financial Scale and Budgetary Analysis of the Mario Abdo Benítez Campaign

The financial scale of the Mario Abdo Benítez campaign in 2018 was characterized by a distinct "dual-track" spending model. The first track consisted of the official party declarations for the general election, while the second track—often much larger and less transparent—encompassed the spending during the internal party primaries and the informal logistical costs on election day.

### Official Budgetary Declarations and State Subsidies

In the 2018 general elections, the ANR secured 1,206,067 votes for the presidential ticket.[6] Under the prevailing system, these votes generated a significant "subsidio electoral," providing the party with millions of dollars in state reimbursements. While official party-wide budgets for the 2018 general election were reported to the TSJE, the total "real" expenditure of the Mario Abdo Benítez campaign is estimated to have far exceeded the official public funding. Investigative reports and subsequent audits of the Abdo Benítez era suggest a highly capitalized operation. For instance, reports surfacing during his presidency indicated that approximately $44 million USD was strategically allocated for advertising pautas aimed at maintaining political narratives and countering opposition influence.[7]

Comparing the 2018 cycle with the 2023 general elections highlights the inflationary nature of Paraguayan politics. In 2023, the ANR declared a budget of approximately $3.84 million USD, which was almost entirely executed.[4] Given the fierce nature of the 2018 internal primary between Mario Abdo Benítez’s "Colorado Añetete" faction and Santiago Peña’s "Honor Colorado" faction, the total mobilization of resources across both phases likely surpassed the 2023 figures. The 2018 primary was essentially a "pre-presidential" election, involving a massive infusion of private capital from party elites and business interests.[2, 4]

The financial power of the ANR in 2018 was not only a result of its voter base but also its historical ability to leverage "recursos institucionales" (institutional resources). Reports from the TSJE and the Ministry of Economy often show that the ANR is the primary beneficiary of state electoral subsidies, receiving over 45% of the total pool of public funds distributed among all political parties.[6]

## Logistical Scale: The Machinery of Territorial Dominance

The logistical scale of the 2018 ANR campaign was vast, reflecting the party’s "hegemonic" status in Paraguayan social and political life. This logistics operation reached its peak on April 22, 2018, when the party deployed a multi-layered infrastructure to ensure that its "disciplined" base of affiliates reached the polling stations.[9]

### The Role of the "Mesarios" and the Polling Infrastructure

In the 2018 elections, the electoral map of Paraguay was divided into thousands of "mesas receptoras de votos." For the 2023 cycle, there were 12,259 such mesas, and the 2018 numbers were comparable.[10] Each mesa is staffed by three official members. The ANR’s logistical goal in 2018 was to achieve 100% saturation, meaning they aimed to place at least one, if not more, of their representatives at every single table across the national territory.

The payment for these "mesarios" is one of the most critical and least documented expenses in Paraguayan politics. According to IDEA International’s diagnostic on political financing, "big parties" like the ANR often pay their table officials significantly more than smaller parties, sometimes up to four times the standard rate, to ensure their presence and vigilance throughout the grueling 12-to-15-hour election day.[4] With over 12,000 mesas, the ANR had to mobilize and pay a core field staff of at least 36,000 "mesarios," supplemented by an equivalent number of "veedores" (observers) and "apoderados" (legal proxies), bringing the total day-of field staff to over 70,000 individuals.[4, 10]

### "Arreo" and the Logistics of Voter Transportation

Perhaps the most significant logistical challenge—and expense—of the Mario Abdo Benítez campaign was the practice of "arreo" (the organized transport of voters). Because the Paraguayan state does not provide free public transportation on election day, the political parties assume this logistical burden.[4] The 2018 campaign utilized a sophisticated fleet of rented buses, private cars, and motorcycles to move voters from rural "compañías" and urban neighborhoods to their designated polling centers.

This operation required:

**Fuel Vouchers:** The distribution of thousands of fuel vouchers to supporters who volunteered their vehicles.

**Karu Guasu:** The provision of food and refreshments (the "great meal") for both the field staff and the transported voters, a practice deeply embedded in the "clientelistic habitus" of the party.[2]

**Local Command Posts:** The establishment of "puestos de comando" near polling stations to coordinate the arrival and departure of vehicles.[11]

The EU Election Observation Mission in 2023 observed that organized transportation was present in 19% of the polling stations they visited, a figure that analysts believe was even higher in the 2018 cycle before more stringent monitoring was introduced.[11]

## The Scale of Campaign Rallies: Territorial Saturation and the "Abrazo Republicano"

The 2018 campaign trail of Mario Abdo Benítez was characterized by a strategy of territorial saturation. The ANR’s victory in 17 out of 18 departments (missing only Alto Paraná) is a testament to the geographic reach of its rally schedule.[8]

### The Transition from Primaries to the General Campaign

The "rallies" of 2018 were split into two distinct strategic phases. The first phase, leading up to the December 2017 primaries, saw Abdo Benítez holding hundreds of smaller, localized "reuniones" with party base leaders (presidentes de seccionales) to build his "Colorado Añetete" movement. The second phase, the general campaign, was defined by the "Abrazo Republicano" (Republican Embrace). This was a tactical reconciliation between Abdo Benítez and his primary rival, Santiago Peña, and the outgoing president, Horacio Cartes.

During this unity phase, the "Lista 1" (the official ballot number for the ANR) held massive rallies in the major urban centers:

**Asunción and Central:** Large-scale closing rallies designed to project an image of party unity and overwhelming force.

**Itapúa and Alto Paraná:** Significant regional events to solidify the agricultural and border-region vote, which are critical for ANR dominance.[8, 12]

While a central repository for the "exact" number of rallies does not exist in public TSJE records, the party’s structure necessitated at least one major event in each of the 250+ municipalities, in addition to the hundreds of smaller neighborhood meetings. The "Lista 1" brand served as a unifying symbol that allowed the party to transcend its internal divisions and present a cohesive front to the national electorate.[12]

## Insights from International Observation Missions (EU and OEA)

The 2018 campaign was under intense scrutiny from international observers, who provided critical insights into the discrepancies between the official narrative and the reality on the ground.

### The EU Election Observation Mission (EU EOM) Findings

The EU EOM Final Report for 2018 highlighted several critical areas of concern regarding the campaign’s integrity. The mission noted that the election was marked by "serious corruption allegations" involving high-level officials, which permeated the campaign narrative.[11] A particularly damaging incident was the "audios filtrados" scandal, where leaked recordings exposed a network of influence peddling and corruption involving politicians and the judiciary.[14] Despite these revelations, many of the implicated figures remained on the lists of candidates and were subsequently elected or re-elected, illustrating the "autoblindaje" (self-protection) of the political class.[14]

The EU mission also documented the following:

**Violence:** There were 415 registered acts of violence against political and social leaders, a decrease from previous years but still a significant factor in the electoral climate.[15]

**Media Imbalance:** The ANR’s financial superiority allowed it to dominate the media space, particularly through paid advertising, which overshadowed smaller parties and coalitions.[11]

### The OAS Observation Mission and Reform Proposals

The OAS mission for the 2018 elections produced 18 specific recommendations for improving the Paraguayan electoral system.[5] Key among these were the need for:

**Registry Updates:** Developing mechanisms to precisely know the domicile of voters and maintaining an updated "Registro Cívico" to prevent fraud.[5]

**Financial Controls:** Implementing real-time monitoring of campaign expenditures, especially during the internal primaries where the bulk of "dark money" enters the system.[3]

**Decriminalization of Defamation:** Encouraging the shift toward civil rather than criminal laws for defamation, in line with international standards on freedom of expression.[11]

The OAS also observed that "assisted voting" was used in 19% of the stations visited in 2023, a practice that in 2018 was often identified as a mechanism for vote-buying or coercion, where party representatives would "assist" voters who did not have a visible disability.[11]

## Second and Third-Order Insights: The Impact of Clientelism and Informality

A deeper analysis of the 2018 Mario Abdo Benítez campaign reveals that the total budget and logistical scale are not just administrative data points; they are symptoms of a "high degree of informality" that characterizes Paraguayan political financing.[4]

### The Causal Relationship Between Logistics and "Shadow" Finance

The most "critical and important" expenses for the election day—transportation and payments to mesarios—are precisely the ones that are hardest to document.[2, 4] Because these transactions are often handled in cash at the local "seccional" level, they rarely appear in the official reports submitted to the TSJE. This creates a "shadow budget" that likely equals or exceeds the declared public financing. The dependency on this shadow budget explains why established parties like the ANR are resistant to reforms that would mandate digital or bank-mediated payments for campaign staff.

### The Institutionalization of Asymmetry

The 2018 campaign demonstrated how the ANR's logistical scale creates an "asymmetry in the competition".[2] By possessing the human resources and the experience to manage complex administrative demands, the ANR effectively crowds out smaller competitors who lack the "maquinaria" (machine) to protect their votes at the mesas.[4] This asymmetry is not just financial; it is organizational and historical. The ANR’s 37.7% of "highly disciplined" affiliates provides a base of support that is structurally more resilient than that of the opposition.[9]

### The Evolution Toward the 2020 Reforms

The flaws exposed during the 2018 Abdo Benítez campaign directly led to the legislative push for Law 6.501/2020. The "lessons learned" from the 2018 cycle—specifically the lack of oversight during the primaries and the impact of the "audios filtrados"—forced the Paraguayan political system to adopt more transparent measures for the 2021 municipal and 2023 general elections.[3] The transition to voting machines (maquinas de votación) and the inclusion of coalitions (concertaciones) in political finance laws were strategic responses to the challenges observed during Mario Abdo’s rise to power.[3, 11]

## Conclusion: The Legacy of the 2018 ANR Campaign

The 2018 presidential campaign of Mario Abdo Benítez stands as a definitive moment in the "post-Stroessner" democratic era of Paraguay. It was a campaign that successfully navigated the internal fissures of the Colorado Party through the "Abrazo Republicano," while leveraging a massive and largely informal logistical machinery to secure victory.

The total budget, while officially reaching into the millions through state subsidies, was in reality supported by a vast network of private and institutional resources that funded the tens of thousands of field staff and the nationwide "arreo" operations. The insights from the EU and OEA reports clarify that while the ANR possesses an unparalleled organizational scale, this scale is often maintained through practices that challenge the principles of electoral equity and transparency.

Ultimately, the 2018 campaign served as both the peak of traditional Colorado mobilization and the catalyst for the modernizing reforms of 2020. The "maquinaria" deployed by Mario Abdo Benítez ensured his presidency, but the controversies it generated—from corruption allegations to the "audios filtrados"—set the stage for a new era of electoral oversight in Paraguay. The 2018 cycle remains a critical reference point for understanding the enduring power of the ANR and the persistent challenges of democratic consolidation in a landscape where money and logistics often speak louder than the ballot itself.
